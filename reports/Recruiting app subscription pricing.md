# Price Recruiting Tracker Below a Single Showcase

Recruiting Tracker should sell one paid plan at **$12.99 a month or $99 a year** (about 36% off the monthly rate), keep today's free web app free, and open the paid plan with a three-week trial. That price lands in a crowded, transparent band: self-serve coach-email apps charge **$9.99-$49.99 a month and $80-$350 a year**, typically with a 17-33% yearly discount and a free tier that rations outreach. The big incumbents sit well above that band. FieldLevel publishes **$29/$49/$79 a month or $278/$470/$758 a year**. CaptainU lists **$22.50-$199.95 a month** with no yearly plan, and BeRecruited still sells **$19.99 a month or $99.99 for life**. SportsRecruits (reportedly **about $99 a month or $399 a year**) and NCSA (reportedly **$1,200-$6,000+**) keep their prices behind a login or a sales call. Baseball's scouting services sell access to rankings and video rather than outreach: PBR's fan tier costs **$20 a month or $120 a year**, Perfect Game's Crosschecker runs **$21.99-$39.99 a month**, and families spend most of their recruiting money on showcases at **$99-$750+ apiece**. At $99 a year, Recruiting Tracker undercuts the closest feature match (NextCommit's $19.99 Varsity tier), matches the most common yearly price among baseball apps ($99.99), costs no more than the cheapest paid PBR showcase entry found ($99), and lines up with the plan's yearly coach-data refresh. The yearly discount is steeper than the 17-20% in-app norm on purpose: recruiting happens in bursts, and annual subscribers are about 2.5 times as likely as monthly ones to still be paying a year later. One caveat applies throughout: no vendor pricing page could be loaded directly, so every figure comes from search-result summaries, and many secondhand prices come from competitors' blogs.

Its **$99.99 lifetime** offer is a legacy anchor that sets a low "pay once" expectation. One competitor's chart lists BeRecruited at $9.99 a month, but the official FAQ's $19.99 should be taken as the real price.

## Subscription data rewards annual plans but warns against over-discounting

General subscription benchmarks back a moderate yearly discount, and they warn against going too deep. RevenueCat says most consumer products discount the annual plan by **17%** compared with twelve months of monthly billing, and cautions that deeper cuts "may sacrifice revenue from folks who would have likely paid more" ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/annual-subscriptions-apps-pros-cons/)). Recurly finds that **16.7%**, or "two months free," is the most popular discount ([Recurly](https://recurly.com/research/saas-benchmarks-for-subscription-plans)). Adapty measures something different: across apps, the median annual plan costs only about **3 times** the median monthly plan, which it describes as "75% savings," and it reports that Education apps discount more than any other category ([Adapty 2025](https://uploads.adapty.io/state_of_in_app_subscriptions_2025.pdf); [Adapty Education](https://adapty.io/blog/education-app-subscription-benchmarks/)). That cross-app ratio partly reflects mobile apps that pair a cheap annual plan with a deliberately expensive monthly one, so it is not a guide to the discount one product should set.

Price-point benchmarks show recruiting apps charging well above education apps in general. RevenueCat's 2026 report puts the median monthly price across all apps at **$10** and the median yearly price at **$34.80** ([RevenueCat SOSA 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)). Education has the highest yearly median of any category, **$44.99**, with a monthly median of $9.99 ([RevenueCat Education](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-education)). Airbridge puts the Education monthly median lower, at $8.38 ([Airbridge](https://www.airbridge.io/en/blog/subscription-app-pricing-by-category-2026-benchmark)). Recruiting tools charge two to five times those medians, which makes sense for a high-stakes purchase that families make for a limited period.

Retention data is the strongest argument for pushing families to the yearly plan. After a year, **33.9% of annual subscribers are still active, compared with 13.8% of monthly subscribers** ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/annual-subscriptions-apps-pros-cons/)). Low-priced annual plans keep up to **36%**, against 6.7% for high-priced monthly plans ([RevenueCat 2025](https://revenuecat.com/blog/company/the-state-of-subscription-apps-2025-launch)). RevenueCat also reports that about 20% of cancelled monthly customers come back within a year, while **95% of annual customers who cancel never return**, and it warns that annual plans can hide weak retention through "delayed churn" ([heise](https://heise.de/-11310636)). Yearly plans already make up 43% of subscriptions in North America ([RevenueCat SOSA 2025](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2025)). Recruiting changes how these numbers should be read. An athlete's need ends at commitment or graduation, so losing an annual customer for good costs little, because that customer would have aged out anyway. Collecting a full season's revenue up front, on the other hand, is worth a lot.

Conversion data sets expectations for how many free users will pay. In Education, the iOS median download-to-paid rate is **3.1%**, and download-to-trial is 6.5% ([RevenueCat Education](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps-2026-education)). Apps with a generous free tier convert at a **2.1% median, against 10.7% for hard paywalls** ([RocketShip HQ](https://www.rocketshiphq.com/revenuecat-state-of-subscription-apps-2025-summary/)). Trial length matters: trials of **17-32 days convert at a 42.5% median, against 25.5% for trials under four days** ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/free-trial-length)). Recruiting Tracker's free web app therefore works as its marketing channel more than as a source of revenue. The paid plan has to be clearly different from the free app, and the trial has to run long enough to show results.

## Charge $12.99 a month or $99 a year for the autopilot

The paid plan adds things that run without the athlete's attention: automatic weekly sending from their own Gmail, automatic detection of coach replies, sync across devices, and a refreshed coach list each year. Those features save time and make results visible, which is what families pay for in this market. At **$12.99 a month or $99 a year**, Recruiting Tracker sits in the lower-middle of the self-serve band, below everything with an incumbent brand behind it.

| Option | Monthly | Yearly | Yearly discount | Yearly price per month | What it signals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lean | $9.99 | $79 | 34% | $6.58 | Matches RecruitReach; easiest yes, thinnest margin, no room left for promotions |
| **Recommended** | **$12.99** | **$99** | **36%** | **$8.25** | 35% below NextCommit Varsity; at the $99.99 yearly anchor for baseball apps |
| Stretch | $14.99 | $119 | 34% | $9.92 | Matches College Recruit AI's monthly price; the yearly price goes over $100 |

The yearly price carries most of the weight. **$99** matches Mind & Muscle's and AthletesGoLive's $99.99 yearly plans, stays under $100, equals the cheapest paid PBR showcase entry found, and works out to $8.25 a month, below the Education monthly median. It also fits the product: the plan includes a yearly coach-data refresh, so one year of the plan buys one season of fresh data. The monthly price is there mainly so the yearly plan looks like the obvious choice. **$12.99** is 35% below NextCommit's $19.99 Varsity tier and less than half of SIGND Pro or FieldLevel Silver at $29. It still sits far enough above $9.99 that the yearly plan reads as about four months free. A family that only wants automation for one summer push still gets a reasonable option. The resulting 36% discount is just above the 17-33% range among competitors and well below PBR's 50% and SportsRecruits' reported 66%. Those are the right limits for a product whose demand comes in bursts and whose customers leave at graduation no matter what.

Where the free tier ends matters as much as the price. Keep every manual feature free and uncapped: the coach list, roster-based emails, two-tap sending, reminders, goals, profile score, and level outlook. That generosity is what separates Recruiting Tracker from FieldLevel's no-first-contact rule, NextCommit's 10 emails a week, and SIGND's three emails a month. It is also what brings in users. Put only automation behind the paywall. Free users keep the coach list they already have, so the yearly data refresh becomes a natural reason to upgrade each fall as coaching staffs change. Offer a **21-day free trial**. That covers three automatic weekly sends, which gives reply detection time to surface a real coach reply, the moment the product proves its value, and it falls within the trial length that converts best. Treat billing terms as part of the pitch: publish the price, allow one-click cancellation, email a reminder before the yearly plan renews, and offer a short refund window on yearly purchases. Each of those answers a specific complaint against NCSA, CaptainU, Perfect Game, or PBR. Because parents usually pay, include a checkout link the athlete can send to a parent. Use limited-time codes, such as a founding-member first year at $79, rather than cutting the list price permanently. That is how University Athlete and Vantage discount. Once high school or travel coaches ask, add a team rate of about **$69 per athlete per year for five or more athletes**. That undercuts FieldLevel's bulk Gold seats at **$79-$139 per athlete** ([NextCommit FieldLevel review](https://www.nextcommit.ai/blog/fieldlevel-review)) and SportsRecruits' $250 club rate.

Skip a lifetime plan. Automatic sending, reply detection, and the yearly data refresh all cost money to keep running, and BeRecruited's $99.99 lifetime offer, unchanged since about 2019, shows how a pay-once price turns into a permanent anchor. Also skip per-email credit tiers and anything that requires a sales call. Three uncertainties could move the numbers. Every competitor price here comes from a search-result summary rather than a loaded page, and several key secondhand figures (SportsRecruits, NCSA, CaptainU's Platinum tier) come from sites that sell competing products. The notes contain no baseball-specific data on what families are willing to pay, so the $12.99 monthly price should be tested against $9.99 while the $99 yearly price stays fixed. Finally, the research did not cover what Google requires of apps that read a user's Gmail, which reply detection depends on. Any fixed compliance cost there should be checked before treating $99 as a floor.

## Conclusion

The main competitor for Recruiting Tracker's paid plan is not NCSA or FieldLevel. It is the athlete doing the work by hand, for free, inside Recruiting Tracker's own free app. Because the free tier already gives away what rivals charge $19.99-$29 a month for, the paid plan cannot sell emails. It has to sell time saved and certainty: knowing that the weekly send went out and that a coach wrote back. That makes reply detection the feature to lead with on the pricing page and to show during the trial, not one item on a feature list. It also means conversion will look like freemium (low single digits), so the free app's reach matters more than the last dollar of price.

Recruiting's fixed time horizon reverses the usual subscription logic. Most apps worry that annual plans hide churn. A recruiting app knows exactly when each customer will leave, so a steeper yearly discount tied to one "season" of refreshed data is both honest and good for revenue. The open question the research could not answer is whether a two-year plan running through junior year would capture even more of the recruiting window, since no recruiting tool sells one yet. Finally, in a category known for sales calls, binding contracts, and charges after cancellation, a tool built by a high school pitcher with published prices and easy cancellation can charge in the middle of the band rather than the bottom. Trust is something families will pay for here.
